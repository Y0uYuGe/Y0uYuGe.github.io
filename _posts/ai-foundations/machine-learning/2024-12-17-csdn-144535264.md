# Fitting
Overfitting: too many parameters (features) — think of it as considering too many factors — or focusing too much on the original training data, making the model overly complex.
Underfitting: too few parameters, focusing too little on the original training data, making the model too simple.

![Image](/images/csdn/2024/csdn-144535264/image-001-670a501c21.png)

As model complexity increases (which can be thought of as the degree of fit to the training data), the training error keeps shrinking, while the generalization error (that is, the error on the test data) first decreases and then increases.

Low, medium, and high complexity correspond to underfitting, good fitting, and overfitting respectively.


# Regularization
Regularization usually works by adding a regularization term to the loss function; this term penalizes model complexity based on the magnitude of the model parameters.
![Image](/images/csdn/2024/csdn-144535264/image-002-43cbe5eab2.png)

# The Regularization Term
![Image](/images/csdn/2024/csdn-144535264/image-003-8b8d93cf2d.png)
L1 and L2 regularization produce different effects on the weights mainly because they penalize the weights in different ways (that is, in how they make the weights change).
### L1 Regularization (Lasso)

L1 regularization adds the sum of the absolute values of the weights to the loss function as a penalty term. This means it penalizes large weight values and also has a significant effect on small weight values. Because the penalty is based on absolute values, when the optimization process tries to minimize the overall loss function, it tends to shrink some weights directly to zero. This is because:

- For a very small weight \( w \), the L1 penalty \( |w| \) is linear in \( w \); even when \( w \) is close to 0, its derivative is still a non-zero constant.
- This gives the optimization algorithm continuous pressure to push these weights further toward 0 when updating them, and they may eventually reach exactly 0.
- Once a weight is set to 0, the L1 penalty no longer affects it (because the absolute value of 0 is still 0), so the weight stays unchanged — equivalent to removing the corresponding feature from the model.

### L2 Regularization (Ridge)

On the other hand, L2 regularization adds the sum of the squared weights to the loss function as a penalty term. This produces the following effects:

- For any non-zero weight \( w \), the L2 penalty \( w^2 \) is always positive and grows rapidly as \( w \) increases.
- However, for \( w \) close to zero, \( w^2 \) grows very slowly, so the L2 penalty has relatively little effect on these small weights.
- During optimization, the L2 penalty puts pressure on all non-zero weights to shrink but not to become zero, so they all become relatively small but usually do not become zero.

In short, L1 regularization encourages sparse solutions (i.e., many weights equal to 0) through the absolute-value penalty, while L2 regularization encourages all weights to take small values rather than become zero through the squared penalty. This difference comes from the different mathematical forms of the two and how they affect the behavior of optimization algorithms such as gradient descent.
