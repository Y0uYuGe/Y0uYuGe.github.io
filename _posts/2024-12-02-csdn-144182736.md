# Symmetric Encryption
Motivation: over an insecure channel, a third party with access can view the communication content
Solution: encrypt with a symmetric cipher. The third party only obtains the ciphertext, which looks like random bits

If both sides use the same key K, encryption and decryption are inverse operations

Important note: the key must be transported between Alice and Bob over a secure channel. A secure channel can be realized, for example, by manually installing the key for the Wi-Fi Protected Access (WPA) protocol or by a human courier. However, the system is only secure if the attacker does not know the key K, which simplifies the secure transmission and storage of K.


# Substitution Cipher
- A historical cipher; a good tool for understanding brute-force attacks and analytical attacks; it encrypts letters rather than bits (like all ciphers before World War II)

![Image](/images/csdn/2024/csdn-144182736/image-002-75722742c2.png)
Brute-force attack (or exhaustive key search)
- Requires (at least) 1 plaintext-ciphertext pair (x0, y0)
- Check all possible keys until the condition is satisfied: dK(y0) = x0 (simply try every possible substitution table until intelligible plaintext appears (note that each substitution table is a key)... How many substitution tables (= keys) are there? 26 × 25 × ... × 3 × 2 × 1 = 26! ≈ 2^88) Searching through 2^88 keys is completely infeasible on today's computers

Why 2^88? Because for a substitution table of 26 letters, each letter can be replaced by any of the other 25 letters, without repetition

- Letter frequency analysis
Letters occur with very different frequencies in English. Moreover: the frequencies of plaintext letters are preserved in the ciphertext. For example, "e" is the most common letter in English; in a typical English text, almost 13% of the letters are "e". The next most common is "t", at about 9%.

   Not only can the frequencies of single letters be used in an attack, but also the frequencies of letter pairs (for example, "th" is very common in English), letter triples, and so on.


Important lesson: even though the substitution cipher has a sufficiently large key space of 2^88, it can easily be defeated by analytical methods. This is a great example showing that an encryption scheme must withstand all types of attacks.


# Why Do We Need Cryptanalysis
Cryptanalysis is essential to modern cryptographic systems: if no one tries to break our cryptographic methods, we will never know whether they are truly secure.

Kerckhoff's principle is crucial in modern cryptography: a cryptosystem should be secure even if the attacker (Oscar) knows all the details of the system except the key.

To realize Kerckhoff's principle in practice: only use well-known ciphers that have been analyzed by excellent cryptographers for several years! (Understanding cryptography only deals with such ciphers.) Note: do not rely on the secrecy of the algorithm to guarantee the security of an encryption system. Although many people may feel that keeping the encryption algorithm secret adds difficulty and makes it more secure, history shows that relying on "secret algorithms" is often dangerous. Once such a "secret algorithm" is reverse-engineered or leaked, the system becomes extremely easy to break. For example, the DVD Content Scramble System (CSS) once tried to protect content through a secret algorithm, but it was eventually successfully reverse-engineered and broken.


# Types of Attacks

### 1. **Ciphertext Only Attack**
   - **Known information**:
     - Encryption algorithm
     - Ciphertext
   - **Description**:
     - In this attack, the attacker only knows the ciphertext and the encryption algorithm, and does not know any corresponding plaintext or the key.
     - The goal of the attack is to derive the plaintext or the key by analyzing the ciphertext.
     - This is the most difficult type of attack, because the least information is known.
![Image](/images/csdn/2024/csdn-144182736/image-003-84f579979e.png)

---
### 2. **Known Plaintext Attack**
   - **Known information**:
     - Encryption algorithm
     - Ciphertext
     - One or more plaintext-ciphertext pairs
   - **Description**:
     - In this attack, the attacker has already obtained some known plaintexts and their corresponding ciphertexts.
     - By analyzing these plaintext-ciphertext pairs, the attacker tries to derive the key of the encryption algorithm or the plaintext corresponding to other undecrypted ciphertexts.
![Image](/images/csdn/2024/csdn-144182736/image-004-9028875cde.png)

---

### 3. **Chosen Plaintext Attack**
   - **Known information**:
     - Encryption algorithm
     - Ciphertext
     - Plaintext chosen by the cryptanalyst together with its corresponding ciphertext
   - **Description**:
     - In this attack, the attacker can choose some plaintexts and obtain their corresponding ciphertexts through the encryption mechanism.
     - By analyzing this data, the attacker tries to derive the key or break the algorithm.
     - This attack assumes that the attacker can partially control the encryption system.
![Image](/images/csdn/2024/csdn-144182736/image-005-821de60b07.png)

---

### 4. **Chosen Ciphertext Attack**
   - **Known information**:
     - Encryption algorithm
     - Ciphertext
     - Ciphertext chosen by the cryptanalyst together with its corresponding decrypted plaintext
   - **Description**:
     - In this attack, the attacker can choose some ciphertexts and obtain their corresponding plaintexts through the decryption mechanism.
     - By analyzing the decrypted data, the attacker tries to derive the key or break the algorithm.
     - This attack assumes that the attacker can partially control the decryption system.
![Image](/images/csdn/2024/csdn-144182736/image-006-a88682e4f4.png)

---


- **Attack difficulty ranking (from hardest to easiest)**:
  1. Ciphertext Only attack
  2. Known Plaintext attack
  3. Chosen Plaintext attack
  4. Chosen Ciphertext attack

# Playfair Cipher
From the keyword, remove the letters that repeat earlier ones in order of appearance, then fill them into the matrix; then take the letters from A to Z that have not appeared before and fill them into the matrix in turn, row by row from left to right
