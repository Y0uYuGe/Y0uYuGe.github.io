# Repeaters
## Functions of a Repeater
The main function of a repeater is to regenerate and restore signals.

A repeater is a network device that works at the physical layer of the OSI model. Its main role is to extend the transmission distance of a network by resending or forwarding data signals. Specifically, the functions of a repeater include:

- Signal regeneration: a repeater can receive a signal, duplicate it, and then send it to another node. During this process, the repeater regenerates the signal to ensure its quality, making it suitable for the next transmission stage.
- Signal restoration: since signals attenuate during transmission, a repeater can amplify these signals to maintain their strength, thereby extending the effective transmission distance of the signal.
Extending network length: through the above functions, a repeater helps extend the length of a network, allowing signals to be transmitted over longer distances and thus expanding the coverage of the network.

## Regenerating Digital Signals vs. Regenerating Analog Signals
The devices that regenerate digital signals are mainly digital repeaters, while the devices that regenerate analog signals are analog amplifiers. Details are as follows:

- Digital repeaters: the main function of a digital repeater is to regenerate and restore digital signals. After receiving and identifying a digital signal, it re-encodes and sends it. This process involves not only amplifying the signal, but more importantly shaping and timing the signal, ensuring that the timing, waveform, and amplitude of the signal elements all meet the specified standards.
- Analog amplifiers: the role of an analog amplifier is to amplify analog signals. Its working principle is to amplify the attenuated signal to compensate for signal loss during transmission. Amplifiers are mainly used for the transmission of analog signals, while repeaters are used for the transmission of digital signals.

## The Same Protocol
The network segments on both ends of a repeater must use the same protocol, because a repeater works at the physical layer of the OSI model; its main function is to amplify and reshape signals, and it does not involve the processing of any higher-layer protocols.

Here are several reasons why the segments on both ends of a repeater must use the same protocol:

- Signal processing: the main role of a repeater is to regenerate signals at the physical level. It receives a signal, amplifies and reshapes it, and then sends it out. This process requires the basic characteristics of the signal, such as the transmission rate and the signal encoding method, to remain consistent.
- Communication standards: network communication standards specify the delay range of signals, and repeaters can only be used within the specified range, otherwise network failures will occur. If the protocols on the two ends are different, the signal may not be correctly recognized and processed, which can cause network failures.
- Device compatibility: the network parts on both ends of a repeater are segments, not subnets. A segment refers to a part of the network that can communicate directly at the physical level, while a subnet involves division at the network layer. As a physical-layer device, a repeater's function is limited to communication within a segment, so the segments on both ends must use the same protocol to ensure device compatibility and network stability.
- Avoiding data loss: if the segments on both ends of a repeater use different protocols, data loss or errors may occur during signal transmission. Because different protocols may have different data transmission rates, frame formats, and other characteristics, these differences will cause the signal to be unable to be correctly parsed and forwarded during transmission (i.e., store-and-forward).
The reason a repeater can connect different media mainly lies in its ability to perform signal conversion between different media.

The basic function of a repeater is to amplify and reshape signals at the physical level in order to extend the transmission distance of signals. Although in most cases the two ends of a repeater connect the same communication medium, some repeaters are designed with the ability to convert signals from one medium to signals of another medium. For example, a repeater may convert an optical fiber signal into a twisted-pair signal. This capability gives repeaters greater flexibility in network design and can meet the needs of different network environments.

However, although repeaters can perform conversion between different media, they are still subject to some limitations:

- Protocol limitation: the segments on both ends of a repeater must use the same protocol, because a repeater does not have the ability to process packets of different protocols.
- Rate limitation: since a repeater works at the physical layer of the OSI model, it cannot connect two LANs with different rates.
- Delay limitation: network standards specify the delay range of signals in detail; a repeater can only work effectively within the specified range, otherwise it will cause network failures.

In general, a repeater can connect different media because it has the ability to convert signals between different media, but this does not mean it can connect any type of network without restriction. In practical applications, the above limiting factors still need to be considered to ensure the stability and performance of the network.

The segments on both ends of a device that does not perform store-and-forward must use the same protocol, because these devices are not designed with the ability to handle differences between different protocols. Details are as follows:

- Signal processing capability: devices that do not perform store-and-forward, such as repeaters and hubs, mainly function to amplify and reshape signals, and do not involve processing at the data link layer or above. Therefore, they cannot recognize or convert signals of different protocols.
- Communication standard requirements: network communication standards specify the delay range of signals; devices such as repeaters can only be used within the specified range, otherwise network failures will occur. If the segments on the two ends use different protocols, the signal may not be correctly recognized and processed, which can cause network failures.
- Technical limitations: these devices can usually only connect homogeneous networks (the same segment), and cannot connect heterogeneous networks (different segments). Because they cannot handle the differences in address systems and underlying hardware interfaces between different network types.
- Performance considerations: the cut-through switching method does not require store-and-forward operations, and can use switch resources more efficiently, improving the throughput of the switch. Store-and-forward switching, on the other hand, requires data storage and forwarding, which occupies switch resources and reduces the throughput of the switch.



The 5-4-3 rule means that between any two computers there can be at most 5 segments, which can include the connecting cables from hub to hub and the connecting cables from hub to computer. This means that during transmission a signal can be forwarded through at most 4 hubs or repeaters. Among these segments, only 3 can be directly connected to computers or other network devices.
- 5 segments: in a network, there can be at most 5 segments. This is due to the technical limitations at the time; more than 5 segments may cause network performance to degrade or become unstable.
- 4 repeaters: the number of repeaters connecting these segments cannot exceed 4. The role of a repeater is to amplify and reshape signals, but too many repeaters may cause signal quality to degrade.
- 3 nodes: among any 3 segments, only 3 nodes can connect devices. This is to ensure the reliability of the network and avoid excessive broadcast traffic.

"Within the specified range" refers to the limit on the count; otherwise, since a repeater needs time to regenerate and restore the signal, and if the signal passes through too many repeaters, more time is needed, resulting in excessive delay and thus network failures.

# Hubs (Multi-port Repeaters)
The main functions of a hub include signal amplification and regeneration, frame broadcasting, and collision detection and handling. Details are as follows:

- Signal amplification and regeneration: a hub can amplify and reshape the received signal, which removes the attenuation that may occur during transmission and ensures that data can be reliably transmitted over longer distances.
- Frame broadcasting: a hub broadcasts the received data frame to all other connected interfaces, which means that all nodes in the LAN can receive the data frame, thereby enabling communication between nodes.
- Collision detection and handling: during network transmission, if multiple nodes send data at the same time, collisions may occur, resulting in data loss. A hub has the ability to detect such collisions and can resolve them through methods such as backoff algorithms, reducing the risk of data loss.

## Why Hubs Cannot Perform Directed Transmission
The design principle of a hub is based on a star topology. After receiving a signal, it amplifies and reshapes the signal, and then broadcasts it to all other connected ports. This broadcast mechanism means that all devices connected to the hub receive the same packet, rather than the packet being selectively transmitted according to its destination address.
## Why a Hub Is a Shared Device
A hub is a shared device because all ports share the same communication channel when it operates.

First, a hub works at the physical layer of the OSI model; its main function is to amplify and reshape signals in order to extend the transmission distance of the network. There is only one channel inside a hub, the common channel, and all ports are connected to this channel. When one port is sending data, the other ports must be in a waiting state, because the hub broadcasts the data to all ports, not just the target port. This design means that at any moment, only one device can use the communication channel for data transmission.

Second, due to the shared nature of a hub, it is inefficient under heavy network load. In a heavily loaded network, multiple devices trying to send data at the same time will cause collisions and packet loss, thereby reducing the overall performance of the network. In contrast, a switch can provide independent bandwidth for each port and can perform directed transmission of packets based on MAC addresses, greatly improving the efficiency and performance of the network.
## Why All Interfaces of a Hub Are in the Same Collision Domain
All interfaces of a hub are within the same collision domain, which is determined by its working mode and design principle.

First, a hub works at the physical layer of the OSI model; its main function is to receive and forward packets, rather than to intelligently forward them according to the destination address of the packet as a switch does. This means that when a device sends data, the hub broadcasts the packet to all other connected devices instead of sending it only to the target device. This broadcast mechanism causes all devices connected to the hub to share the same communication medium, so they are all in the same collision domain. If two or more devices send data at the same time, a collision may occur — the packets interfere with each other during transmission, causing data loss or corruption.

Second, a collision domain refers to a network area in which a collision may occur if two devices transmit data at the same time. In a hub, since all devices share the same communication medium, the entire network is regarded as one collision domain. This means that at the same moment only one device can communicate, otherwise a collision will occur. In addition, a hub does not have the ability to isolate broadcast domains, because all ports are in the same broadcast domain, and any broadcast packet will be sent to all ports.


Bandwidth sharing: if hosts must communicate at the same time, the working hosts share the bandwidth equally — the bandwidth of each hub broadcast stays the same, but it is made up of data sent by each working host, and each of them gets an equal share of the bandwidth. At this point the transmission efficiency is very low, because it is an equal split of the original bandwidth.

# Summary
Hubs and repeaters are two common network hardware devices that play different roles in a network. Details are as follows:

- Repeater: a repeater works at the physical layer of the OSI model; its main function is to receive signals from the network, amplify and reshape them, and then send them out, thereby extending the transmission distance of the signals. A repeater usually has only two ports, used to connect two sections of network cable. Its use follows the "5-4-3 rule", that is, in a network there can be at most 5 segments and 4 repeaters, and only 3 of the segments can be used to connect hosts.
- Hub: a hub also works at the physical layer, but unlike a repeater, it is a multi-port forwarder that can broadcast the received signal to all other ports. A hub usually has 4 to 20 or more ports, used to connect multiple devices. A hub does not have intelligent forwarding capability; that is, it does not know to which specific device a packet should be sent, and simply sends the packet to all connected devices.

In general, repeaters are mainly used for signal enhancement and extending transmission distance, while hubs are used to connect multiple network devices and broadcast signals to all connected devices.
