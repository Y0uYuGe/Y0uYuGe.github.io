# Baseband Signals and Broadband Signals

Signals transmitted over a channel can be divided not only into digital and analog signals, but also into baseband and broadband signals — just a different classification criterion

Baseband signal: the original electrical signal that has not been modulated, i.e. the signal from the source; it can be either a digital or an analog signal

Baseband transmission: transmission over a digital channel is baseband transmission (what is transmitted is generally a digital signal)

Broadband signal: after the baseband signal is modulated, its frequency becomes higher — that is a broadband signal (modulation lets it be placed on complex, hostile channels; for example, sound is modulated by a microphone, raising its frequency so it can withstand interference from various signals, allowing the receiver to filter out the original baseband signal)

Broadband transmission: transmission over an analog channel is broadband transmission

Attenuation is caused by signal interference. Baseband signals generally have low frequencies, starting near zero frequency. So if the attenuation is too great the signal may simply vanish. Hence baseband is for short distances

Manchester encoding

The transition serves as the clock signal: every symbol sent produces one transition, so the receiver knows a symbol has been sent, and then derives the symbol's value from the signal before and after the transition

Each original symbol is split into two parts, a low-level and a high-level symbol, so the frequency doubles and the corresponding frequency range doubles; hence the passband width is twice the original baseband width
**Symbol width is the time required to transmit one symbol, while passband width and baseband width refer to the frequency range occupied by the signal**
> Baseband width and passband width are two important concepts in communication systems; they represent different signal transmission methods and system design parameters. Details:
- Baseband width: usually refers, in digital communication, to the frequency range occupied by the digital signal. A baseband signal is the spectrum of the information signal itself, such as the binary data signal produced by a computer. Baseband transmission sends the digital signal directly over a cable or other medium without modulation, so the baseband width is the spectral width of those digital signals.
- Passband width: also called bandwidth; in analog communication it is the frequency range occupied by the modulated signal. Passband transmission modulates the baseband signal onto a higher frequency for transmission, in order to achieve multiplexing on the channel, increase channel capacity, and reach longer transmission distances.

> 
> The passband width of Manchester encoding is twice the original baseband width because of the inherent characteristics of Manchester encoding.
- First, Manchester encoding is a biphase encoding: it encodes each data bit into two opposite levels — a logical "0" is represented by a positive pulse followed by a negative pulse, while a logical "1" is represented by a negative pulse followed by a positive pulse. This means there is a level transition within every symbol period, whether from positive to negative or negative to positive. This transition provides self-synchronization because it carries clock information, and the receiver can synchronize its reading of the data by detecting these transitions.
- Second, since each symbol contains two different signal states, this effectively doubles the frequency of the baseband signal (you can think of the symbol rate doubling). In digital communication, the baud rate (symbol rate) is the number of symbols the transmitting equipment can send per second; in Manchester encoding, because each data bit is encoded into two different level states, the baud rate is twice the data rate. This is also why the passband width occupied by a Manchester-encoded signal is twice the original baseband width.
- Finally, the increased passband width means wider spectrum resources are needed for transmission, which can be a waste of resources. However, Manchester encoding's advantages lie in its good noise immunity and self-synchronization capability, which make it very useful in certain application scenarios, especially in noisy environments or situations with strict timing requirements.
> In summary, Manchester encoding does increase the required passband width by adding an extra transition to every data bit, but it also provides other advantages, such as self-synchronization and noise immunity.

# Encoding Analog Data into a Digital Signal
The sampling theorem: any analog signal is composed of a series of sine waves

Quantization: the sampled points are converted into integer values according to a grading standard

> Frequency is the number of periodic changes per unit time, usually expressed in Hertz (Hz).

The higher the sampling frequency, the more accurately the waveform can be reconstructed
It is like deriving a sine or cosine function from the sampled points: if the sampling frequency is twice the signal frequency, you can sample exactly four points per quarter period and reconstruct the waveform fairly completely; if the sampling frequency equals the original frequency, the collected points cannot distinguish the waveform

# Modulating Analog Data into an Analog Signal

More precisely, this is amplify-and-modulate, because the modulated analog signal may suffer some loss during transmission and therefore needs amplification — raising the frequency

![在这里插入图片描述](/images/csdn/2024/csdn-135852023/image-012-7c2233516c.png)
Modulating analog data into an analog signal involves combining the original analog information (such as sound or images) with a high-frequency carrier signal using specific techniques, so the signal can be transmitted effectively. Details:

- Amplitude Modulation (AM): the analog data is represented by varying the carrier's amplitude. In AM, the carrier's frequency and phase remain unchanged while its amplitude varies with the analog signal.
- Frequency Modulation (FM): the analog data is represented by varying the carrier's frequency. In FM, the carrier's amplitude remains unchanged while its frequency varies with the strength of the analog signal.
- Phase Modulation (PM): the analog data is represented by varying the carrier's phase. In PM, the carrier's amplitude and frequency remain unchanged while its phase varies with the analog signal.
- In addition, the modulation process can be viewed as a spectrum shift: the spectrum of the baseband signal (the original analog signal) is moved to a higher frequency. The purpose is to exploit the characteristics of different frequency bands to achieve longer-distance transmission or to transmit multiple signals simultaneously over the same communication medium.
